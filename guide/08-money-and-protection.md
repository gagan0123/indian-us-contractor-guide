# Money and protection: what your employer used to do for you

As an employee, four things happened without you: tax was deducted each
month, a group health policy covered you, PF was saved for you, and the
salary arrived on the same date in the same amount. As a contractor none
of them happens unless you set it up. Do this chapter **before your last
working day**, because two of the items are much easier while you still
have a salary slip.

## Health insurance

- **Group cover ends on your last working day**, or at the end of that
  month, depending on the policy. Ask HR for the exact date in writing.
- Many group policies let you **convert to an individual policy** with
  the same insurer and carry over the waiting-period credit. Ask HR
  before you leave: the window to convert is usually short (often 30
  days around exit).
- If you buy fresh, buy **before** the group cover ends, so there is no
  gap. A new retail policy has waiting periods (commonly 30 days for
  illness, two to four years for pre-existing conditions), and a gap
  restarts nothing you can get back.
- A base policy plus a **super top-up** is usually the cheapest way to a
  large cover. Cover yourself, your spouse and children; insure parents
  on a separate policy so their claims do not exhaust yours.
- Premiums paid for yourself, spouse, children and parents are
  deductible under the **old tax regime only** (old s. 80D; the limits are
  in `01-income-tax.md`). Under the new regime there is no deduction,
  which does not make the cover less necessary.

## Term life insurance

- Employer group life cover ends with the job too.
- **Buy term cover while you still have salary slips and a Form 16.**
  Insurers underwrite a self-employed applicant on ITRs, and in your
  first contractor year you have no contractor ITR to show. Many will ask
  for two or three years of returns, or offer a lower cover.
- A common rule of thumb is cover of 10 to 15 times annual income, plus
  outstanding loans, running to your planned retirement age.

## Retirement savings after EPF

Employer PF stops. What happens to the existing balance, and its tax, is
in `07-leaving-employment.md`. To replace the habit:

- **PPF**: 15-year account, tax-free interest, up to ₹1.5 lakh a year.
  The deposit is deductible under the old regime (old s. 80C).
- **NPS (Tier I)**: market-linked, locked to 60. An extra ₹50,000
  deduction under the old regime (old s. 80CCD(1B)). The employer
  contribution route (80CCD(2)) is closed to you now.
- **Your own investments**: a monthly SIP sized to what PF used to take
  (employee plus employer share was 24 % of basic) is a simple target.

## Cash flow: make the income regular yourself

- Keep **one bank account for the work**: Deel credits come in, tax and
  business expenses go out, and a fixed monthly transfer goes to your
  personal account. It makes the books and the CA's job easy, and a loan
  officer reads it as a salary.
- **Set aside for tax on every credit.** Move a fixed percentage of each
  credit to a separate account or a liquid fund, to pay advance tax.
  `01-income-tax.md` has the numbers; for many people on the presumptive
  scheme 15 to 20 % of each credit covers it, and more at higher incomes.
  The calculator in the guide page gives your figure.
- **GST on exports under an LUT is nil**, so there is nothing to set
  aside for it; only the cost of filing.
- **Emergency fund: 6 to 12 months of expenses**, larger than an
  employee needs, because a contract can end on 15 or 30 days' notice and
  there is no notice pay, no severance and no unemployment benefit.
- **Exchange rate**: if the contract is in USD, your rupee income moves
  with the rate every month. Budget on a slightly worse rate than today.
- **Leave and holidays are unpaid** unless the contract says otherwise.
  Price them into your rate.
